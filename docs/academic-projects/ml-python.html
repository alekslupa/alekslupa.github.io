The central argument: for imbalanced business classification problems the decision rule, not the choice of classifier, is often the more consequential design lever.

About the research

This was my final research paper for the BAA1027 Machine Learning module, supervised by Dr Michael Farayola. Unlike my group projects, this was a solo piece of work from dataset selection through to final submission. The dataset is the Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention collection published by Sakar et al. (2019) and distributed through the UCI Machine Learning Repository: 12,330 online sessions described by 17 features and a binary Revenue target, with a moderate 15.5% positive class imbalance.

The research question was whether a standard comparative classifier pipeline could be meaningfully extended by three additions: K-means cluster labels injected as an engineered feature, a soft voting ensemble, and cost-sensitive threshold optimisation parameterised from published e-commerce economics. The empirical answer, reported honestly including the two innovations that did not work, produced a more interesting finding than if everything had worked: the decision rule matters more than the classifier.

1. Classifier benchmark

Five classifiers were tuned via GridSearchCV with 5-fold stratified cross-validation, scoring on F1 rather than accuracy to reflect the class imbalance. XGBoost recorded the highest F1 (66.73%) and PR-AUC (74.53%), narrowly ahead of Random Forest. SVM and Logistic Regression lagged materially, the former on recall, the latter because a linear decision surface cannot model the non-linear PageValues saturation effect.

The Voting Classifier illustrates a counterintuitive result: it aggregates the four base learners via soft probability voting and achieves the highest precision of any model (77.64%), but it loses recall relative to XGBoost alone (44.93% vs 61.36%). The net effect is an F1 of 56.92%, ten points below XGBoost. Cornelio et al. (2021) show that voting ensembles produce marginal gains only when base learners have complementary failure modes. On this dataset they do not, so the ensemble is reported as a negative result rather than buried in the appendix.

2. Generalisation: XGBoost vs Random Forest

On headline test-set F1, XGBoost and Random Forest look comparable. The train versus test comparison tells a materially different story. Random Forest achieved a perfect training F1 of 1.000 against a test F1 of 0.621, a 0.38 gap indicating severe overfitting despite the F1-scored grid search. XGBoost, by contrast, produced a 0.04 gap between training and test performance.

The Random Forest grid search converged on unlimited max_depth and single-sample leaves, which explains the perfect training fit. In a production setting, tighter regularisation via a constrained depth search or a higher min_samples_split would be worth exploring. For the purposes of this paper, the clean XGBoost gap argues for it as the production model of choice regardless of the narrow test-set margin.

3. The cost-sensitive threshold innovation

This is the paper’s central methodological contribution and its strongest empirical result. The default 0.5 classification threshold presumes symmetric misclassification costs, which is rarely appropriate in e-commerce. I derived a cost ratio from two published benchmarks: Littledata (2025) reports USD 92 average revenue per Shopify customer; WordStream (2025) reports USD 19.68 average Facebook Ads cost per acquisition. The ratio of 4.67 was rounded up to 5 to reflect a deliberate preference toward minority-class recall, then stress-tested across the 3:1 to 7:1 range.

Two results emerge from the sensitivity analysis. First, the optimal threshold is stable at 0.16 across ratios 3:1 through 6:1, shifting only modestly to 0.11 at 7:1. The threshold optimisation is robust to moderate misspecification of the cost ratio, which matters because the Littledata and WordStream benchmarks are US-based while the dataset is of unknown geography. Second, the cost reduction scales monotonically from 16.5% at the most conservative ratio to 41.9% at the most aggressive. Even at 3:1 the adjustment delivers double-digit savings.

Code 
import numpy as np
import plotly.graph_objects as go

# Illustrative curve reconstructed from the paper's cost function
# Total cost = 5 * FN + 1 * FP under the 5:1 cost ratio
thresholds = np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 91)

# Smooth U-shape with minimum around 0.16, calibrated to paper figures
# Default (0.5) cost = 1234, optimum (0.16) cost = 825
def cost_curve(t):
    base = 825 + 3200 * (t - 0.16) ** 2
    if t < 0.16:
        base += 2400 * (0.16 - t) ** 1.4
    return base

costs = np.array([cost_curve(t) for t in thresholds])

fig = go.Figure()

fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(
    x=thresholds, y=costs,
    mode="lines",
    line=dict(color="#1e7e34", width=3),

    fillcolor="rgba(30,126,52,0.10)",

))

The 33.1% cost reduction at the derived 5:1 ratio is substantially larger than the F1 difference between the best and worst classifiers in the baseline comparison. This is the basis for the paper’s central argument: for imbalanced business classification problems, the translation of business economics into a decision rule delivers more practitioner value than incremental improvements in classifier accuracy.

4. What the threshold shift actually does

The cost reduction on its own is a summary statistic. The confusion-matrix shift underneath it shows the operational trade-off: at the optimal threshold the model catches far more positive cases but at the price of more false alarms. This is the right trade-off for an e-commerce retargeting use case where the cost of missing a potential converter (lost revenue) substantially exceeds the cost of showing an advert to a non-converter.

The key cell is the false-negative count. At the default threshold, 221 genuine buyers were classified as non-buyers, representing either missed retargeting opportunities or under-prioritised sessions in a live recommendation system. At the optimal threshold that figure drops to 82, recovering 139 previously misclassified converters. The cost of doing so is 286 additional false positives, but under the 5:1 cost ratio this is a favourable trade by design.

5. Feature importance: PageValues dominates

Two independent interpretability methods were used. Random Forest’s built-in feature importance ranks PageValues first by a wide margin. SHAP TreeExplainer on the tuned XGBoost model returns the same ordering. Two different aggregation principles and two different classifier families produce convergent rankings, which is stronger evidence for the behavioural importance of PageValues than either method in isolation would provide.

The behavioural features (green) dominate the ranking. The innovation is reported as a negative result in the paper: methodologically sound, empirically redundant.

6. The analytical pipeline

The research followed a pipeline from raw data through to interpretability, with cost-sensitive threshold optimisation slotted in after classification rather than before. This matters because it is the point where business economics enters the analytical chain, and placing it after rather than before the classifier keeps the upstream model training decoupled from downstream operational parameters.

Limitations acknowledged in the paper

WarningFour limitations worth flagging

  1. Single platform, single time window. The dataset reflects one e-commerce platform and a twelve-month collection window, which constrains generalisability.

  2. Geographical mismatch on the cost ratio. The Littledata and WordStream benchmarks are US-based, while the dataset geography is unknown. The sensitivity analysis in Section 3 directly mitigates this concern by showing threshold stability across 3:1 to 7:1.

  3. Single stratified split. Evaluation relies on one train/test partition, leaving metrics subject to sampling variance. k-fold test evaluation would tighten confidence intervals.

  4. Scaler fitted on full dataset. The MinMaxScaler was applied to the full frame before the train/test split rather than fitted on the training partition only. The deterministic bounded nature of Min-Max scaling makes material impact unlikely, but the pattern should be corrected in production.

Reflection

This was the most technically demanding piece of work of my degree. The core lesson was methodological rather than technical. Going into the project I expected the interesting finding to be which classifier won. What actually emerged was that the classifier choice mattered much less than the decision rule applied on top of it. The 33.1% cost reduction from the threshold shift is larger than the F1 gap between any two classifiers in the baseline comparison, which is a result I did not anticipate and which changed how I think about applied machine learning.

Reporting the two negative results honestly was a deliberate choice and the one that took most intellectual adjustment. K-means cluster injection and the soft voting ensemble were both methodologically defensible ideas when I designed them. Neither worked. The temptation to either bury them in the appendix or find a metric that made them look marginally favourable was real. Instead I kept them in the main narrative with an explicit framing as negative results and a reasoned explanation of why they failed (the tree ensembles had already extracted the available signal from the raw features, and the base learners lacked the complementary failure modes that voting ensembles depend on). Reading the resulting paper back, the negative results strengthen rather than weaken the overall argument, because they locate the positive result (cost-sensitive thresholding) more precisely in methodological space.

The broader takeaway I carried out of the module is that machine learning as a discipline is closer to applied statistics with a business objective than to computer science with a model zoo. The classifier is the smaller part of the problem. The decision rule, the evaluation design, the limitation audit and the feature interpretability are the parts that determine whether the model is useful or misleading. This is the lens I intend to apply in business analyst roles where similar risks apply: accuracy-optimising a model against a dataset that is not the real production distribution is the easiest way to produce an impressive-looking artefact that fails in deployment.

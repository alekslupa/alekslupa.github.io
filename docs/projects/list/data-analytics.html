A 10-year diagnostic and predictive analytics engagement on EcoEnergy Corp, a global sustainable infrastructure and technology company operating across five continents. The team’s brief was to identify the drivers of the company’s €8.46M net loss and recommend a restructuring path back to profitability. The deliverable was a Power BI consulting suite covering descriptive, diagnostic and predictive layers, supported by a 1,904-word written report.

About the project

EcoEnergy Corp is a global sustainable infrastructure and technology company selling across three categories (Technology, Construction, Transport & Insurance) in five regions. The dataset covered 10 years of sales, costs, channels and discount rates with sufficient granularity to support both regional diagnostics and product-level recommendations.

Group 4 was structured as a regional consulting team. Each member led the analysis for one region: I covered Asia, with the other four regions allocated to Niall Grainger (North America), Roisin Murphy (Europe), Daniel Vala (Africa) and Martinas Jucys-Brady (South America). The cross-regional synthesis (outsourcing strategy, predictive forecast, industry benchmarking) was developed collectively. This page reproduces the team’s collective findings, with the Asia section reflecting analysis I led directly.

NoteWhat’s on this page

The visualisations below reproduce the core findings from our Power BI consulting deliverable as interactive Plotly charts. The original deliverable was a multi-page Power BI report with a star-schema data model, DAX KPIs, drill-through navigation and a forecasting layer. This page rebuilds the same underlying analysis as executable Python with Plotly for the portfolio. All numerical results originate from the dataset and Power BI report submitted for assessment.

1. The portfolio is failing

Before any regional or product analysis, the topline numbers established the scale of the problem. EcoEnergy generated €130.7M in revenue across the 10-year window but incurred €139.1M in costs, producing a €8.46M cumulative net loss. The portfolio breakdown is more revealing than the top line: 39% of all products were sold at a loss, and 8 of the 10 product lines were collectively unprofitable across the period.

The interpretation that matters analytically is that the loss is not a market problem (revenue exists) but a portfolio composition and cost discipline problem. That framing shaped every subsequent diagnostic decision: the question was not “how do we sell more”, it was “which products and regions should we keep, drop, or restructure”.

2. Regional performance scorecard

Aggregating to the regional level surfaces five distinct patterns. North America’s losses were the largest in absolute terms but the cause (sub-cost pricing on key product lines) was clear. Africa was loss-making with high cost as the structural driver. Europe was profitable in some categories but volatile. South America was a low-cost manufacturing leader but with weak Insurance and Transport performance. Asia is the case I analysed most closely, and the finding there is the one I want to spend most time on.

3. Asia deep-dive: revenue is not profit

This was my regional analysis. Asia generated €24.05M in revenue across the decade, the highest of any region, but produced only €0.14M in net profit. That is a 0.6% margin on the most active geography in the portfolio. The diagnostic question was why such strong topline activity translated into almost no margin.

Code

The 2018 to 2023 period coincided with two compounding decisions: aggressive online discounting (Asia’s online channel dominates the regional sales mix) and continued investment in product lines that were structurally unprofitable for the region (flight tickets, insurance, printers, certain tech devices). The 2024 recovery is what happens when those decisions reverse: the team narrowed to the lines where Asia has genuine cost-and-demand fit (cars, solar panels, smartphones, tablets, laptops) and tightened discount discipline.

TipThe Asia lesson generalises

The phrase “high revenue, near-zero profit” is a useful diagnostic flag for any consumer business. It almost always indicates that the discounting structure is funding the volume rather than the margin. The fix is unglamorous: enforce minimum margin thresholds, exit the perennial loss-makers, and accept smaller revenue numbers as the price of real profit. Asia’s 2024 numbers show the trade is worth making.

4. Where production should actually happen

The team’s central commercial recommendation was an outsourcing strategy based on regional cost advantage at the product level. The “winner-takes-all” sourcing principle (Jain, 2024) directs production to the region with the lowest median unit cost for each product, provided that region has demonstrable cost discipline. Median was preferred over mean to suppress outlier sales records that distorted the production cost picture.

Code

The recommendation collapses to: Technology → South America and Asia, Construction (building materials) → Europe (because it leads on demand and was the only profitable Construction region), Solar panels → South America (cheapest at €273.42 per unit), Cars and flight tickets → North America, Insurance plans → Europe. Transport and Insurance categories with no defensible regional fit should be exited entirely rather than restructured.

5. Predictive forecast: from €8.46M loss to €3.09M profit

The predictive layer asked whether the outsourcing recommendation would actually restore profitability if implemented. The forecast applied three conservative adjustments to the cost base: production hub unit costs replaced regional costs, shipping costs were multiplied by 5 to reflect longer-distance logistics from consolidated hubs, and capacity was capped at 50% in Year 1 and 75% in Year 2 to model realistic ramp.

Code

    "Margin":   [49.7, 49.6, 49.7],

Technology accounts for €7.75M of the €15.6M Year 3 revenue forecast, making it the single largest category and the one most sensitive to the outsourcing assumption holding. Construction earns €2M but with weaker margins, raising a follow-on question of whether some Construction lines should also be exited despite Europe’s demand strength. Predictive analytics that produces follow-on diagnostic questions is doing its job; predictive analytics that produces only confidence is usually doing the opposite.

6. The analytical pipeline

The three layers of the engagement (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive) follow a deliberate sequence. Each layer is constrained to questions the data can actually answer at its level of resolution, and recommendations are produced only at the predictive layer, after the diagnostic has surfaced the causal structure.

Reflection

This was the first project of my degree where the analytical work was the entire point of the deliverable. Strategy projects use frameworks; new enterprise projects use financial models; this was a genuine “make sense of a real dataset and produce a defensible recommendation” engagement. The Power BI deliverable forced a discipline I had not yet developed properly: the figure exists to support the argument, not the other way around. Several of my early Asia drafts had charts that were technically accurate but did not advance the story. Cutting those was harder than producing them.

The Asia finding (highest revenue, near-zero profit) was the moment the dataset taught me something I would not have arrived at by reasoning alone. Going in I had assumed the worst-performing regions would be the obvious laggards on revenue. The truth was the opposite: the region generating the most activity was barely breaking even, because the activity was being subsidised by discounts. That pattern (high revenue masking weak unit economics) is one I now look for in any commercial dataset. I came across it again in a different form during my Machine Learning research paper, where the cost-sensitive threshold work surfaced a similar lesson about apparent performance hiding underlying structural problems.

Working in a five-person consulting team was also instructive. The regional ownership structure (one analyst per continent) gave each member a defensible domain but introduced a real coordination challenge: ensuring the cross-regional synthesis chapters did not contradict the regional ones. We resolved this by having the regional leads peer-review each other’s outsourcing recommendations before they were finalised, which caught two material inconsistencies that would otherwise have shipped. The skills matrix approach we used later in BAA1019 came directly from this engagement.

Rebuilding the Power BI exhibits as Plotly charts for this portfolio was a separate exercise from the module itself. Power BI is the right tool for a recurring corporate dashboard; Plotly is the right tool for a static, narrative-driven portfolio page. Translating between them forced the same discipline as the original analytical work: the figure exists to support the argument, and any chart that does not pass that test should be cut. The final six visualisations on this page are what survived that filter, out of approximately eighteen in the original Power BI report.
